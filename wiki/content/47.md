# Segmentation

Split a mesh into meaningful parts — limbs off a torso, armour plates off a body, a handle off a blade — and export them as one GLB with a separate object per part. The split is found by measuring how **thick** the shape is everywhere and where its **creases** are, which is what makes it work on the organic, generator-produced meshes this editor mostly handles, where curvature alone finds nothing.

Segmentation **never modifies the mesh**. There is no Keep/Revert: the parts are written to a separate file, and the mesh in the editor is untouched throughout.

> The analysis runs on the **Python mesh-tools service**. Make sure it is configured and running in **Settings → Mesh Tools**. In the desktop app the service is started for you.

## How it works

The service estimates the **Shape Diameter Function**: from every face it fires rays into the volume and measures the distance to the far wall. That is a measure of local thickness — a limb is thin, a torso is thick, and the step between them is where a human would cut.

Faces are then merged greedily, cheapest pair first, into a full merge history. The cost of a merge mixes the thickness difference with the fold across the shared edge, biased so that cutting along a **concave crease** is far cheaper than cutting over a **convex bulge** — a boundary belongs in a valley, never across a ridge.

Nothing on the service decides how many parts you get. The **whole merge history** comes back, and the editor replays it locally. That is why the Parts slider is instant and never contacts the service again.

## Running it

1. Open the **Segmentation** mode in the left toolbar.
2. Click **Analyze Mesh**. This is the only step that costs a round trip — run it once.
3. Drag **Parts** (or click **Auto**) until the split looks right.
4. Correct anything the analysis got wrong with **Fix By Hand**.
5. Set **Min part faces**, choose whether to keep the texture, and click **Export**.

After the analysis, a summary line reports what was measured: the proxy face count, the number of separate shells, and how many of the mesh's faces were mapped onto the proxy. Two warnings can appear under it:

- **"n% of the thickness rays escaped"** (above 35%) — the mesh is open, so the rays fly out through the holes instead of hitting a far wall, and the thickness measurement is unreliable. The parts are then guessed from creases alone. Repair the mesh first (the Repair controls live in the **[Auto Retopo](30.md)** panel) for a better split.
- **"Thickness was measured on the decimated proxy"** — the mesh is too dense to ray-cast at full resolution because the **embreex** accelerator is not installed on the service. Thin details may be under-separated.

## Parts

**Parts** chooses where to cut the merge history. It is free to move — nothing is recomputed and the service is not contacted — so the fastest way to find the right split is simply to drag it.

The slider stops at the number of regions the analysis actually has: it can never exceed the proxy face count (or 200, whichever is lower), and below a certain number the remaining pieces are separate **shells** that are not connected to one another at all. When that floor is reached the panel says *only n regions available*.

**Auto** jumps to the most natural stopping point: the part count where the next merge suddenly becomes much more expensive than the last one — every merge below it was cheap, meaning those pieces belonged together, and the one above it was not. It is scored on how much that boundary is actually worth keeping, not on the size of the jump alone, so it lands on real structure rather than on an anomaly deep in the tail. On a mesh with too little hierarchy to judge, the button is disabled.

**Explode** pushes the parts apart so you can see inside the split. It is **preview only** — the mesh, and everything you export, are untouched. **Reassemble** puts it back.

> While the parts are exploded the correction tools are disarmed. The pickers raycast the mesh where it really is, not where the exploded preview draws it, so a click would land on a face you never aimed at.

## Fix By Hand

Three tools for the boundaries the analysis put in the wrong place. All of them store their correction **against the faces themselves**, never against a part number — so a correction survives moving the Parts slider, merging, splitting, and every later change to the part count.

### Brush — sweep faces into another part

1. **Click the part you want the faces moved INTO.** The swatch in the panel shows the target colour.
2. **Drag** over the faces that should join it.

| Action | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| **Drag** | Sweep the faces under the brush into the target part. |
| **Ctrl + drag** | Release those faces back to whatever the analysis decided. |
| **Shift + click** | Choose a different target part. |

**Brush size** is a radius in model units. Back faces are never painted, so a dab on a thin limb cannot silently reassign the other side of it. **Undo Stroke** takes back the last stroke; **Clear All Strokes** drops every brushed correction at once.

### Merge — fuse parts together

Click parts to gather them; click one again to drop it from the selection. The rest of the mesh dims so the selection is unambiguous. With two or more picked, **Fuse n Parts** merges them into one. **Reset Merges** undoes all of them.

### Split One — cut inside a single part

Raising **Parts** re-cuts the *whole* model, which is the wrong tool when only one part is under-segmented. Split One fixes exactly that:

1. Click the part you want cut up. Everything else is **frozen** as it is.
2. Raise **Parts**. Each step up is now one more cut **inside that part only** — every other part comes out bit-for-bit identical.
3. **Apply & Pick Next** bakes the proposed cuts in and lets you move on to another part. **Cancel Focus** abandons them.

Once a split has been applied, the hierarchy level is **pinned** and the Parts slider is locked while nothing is focused — every other part has to stay exactly as it is for the applied cuts to mean anything. Use Split One on another part, or **Reset Splits** to go back to the whole model.

## Analysis options

Changing any of these shapes the hierarchy itself, so they need another **Analyze**. The defaults segment a typical character or prop sensibly — reach for these when the result is wrong in a specific way, not as a matter of course.

| Field | Default | What it does & how to tune it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Proxy faces** | 3,000 | The detail the analysis runs at. Everything downstream is superlinear in this number, so higher means finer boundaries and a **much** slower analysis. Raise it when a part is missing because it is small relative to the whole mesh. |
| **Crease bias** | 0.12 | How much cheaper it is to cut in a valley than over a ridge. **Lower it** when boundaries cut across a bulge they should have gone around; **1.0** ignores the distinction entirely. |
| **Thickness weight** | 1.0 | How strongly parts are separated by how thick they are — a limb from a torso. |
| **Crease weight** | 1.0 | How strongly parts are separated by the fold along their shared boundary — a panel line. |
| **Thickness rays** | 20 | Rays per proxy face. More is steadier on noisy surfaces, and slower. |
| **Precise thickness** | On | Cast the rays against the **full-resolution** mesh instead of the proxy. This is the only way thin details read as thin — leave it on. On a dense mesh it falls back to the proxy automatically when the service has no **embreex** accelerator, and says so. |

When parts are separated by the *wrong criterion*, the two weights are the fix: raise **Thickness weight** for a body whose limbs are not coming off, raise **Crease weight** for a hard-surface model whose panel lines are being ignored.

## Output

| Field | Default | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Min part faces** | 4 | Parts with fewer faces than this are dropped from the export — decimation leaves a scatter of one- and two-triangle regions that are noise, not parts. The panel reports how many fall below the line. |
| **Keep the mesh texture** | On | Export the parts with the mesh's own texture instead of their preview colours. Only shown when the mesh actually has one. |

**Export n Parts** downloads `<mesh>-parts.glb`: a single file containing one named object per part — `<mesh>_part_01`, `<mesh>_part_02`, and so on — so the pieces stay separable wherever the file lands, which is the entire point of having segmented it.

### Keeping the texture

The parts keep the UVs they had as part of the whole mesh, so they can share the mesh's texture directly — **nothing is re-baked, re-projected or resampled**, and what you get is exactly what the editor is showing you. Every part references the one image, and it is stored in the GLB exactly once however many parts there are, so the file is no larger than the mesh's own texture.

Turn it **off** to get each part in the flat colour it is drawn in on screen instead, which is what makes the split readable in a viewer that shows you nothing but the shape. A mesh with no texture — or one with UVs but nothing painted onto them yet — always exports as flat colours, and the toggle is hidden.

> Parts are exported as plain meshes: a **skeleton and its skin weights do not travel with them**. Segment before rigging, not after.

**Clear Segmentation** drops the analysis and returns the viewport to the normal shaded view. Your hand corrections go with it.

## Notes

- The analysis is indexed **by triangle number**, so it is only valid for the exact mesh it was run on. Any edit that changes the triangle count — sculpting, **[Optimize / LOD](31.md)**, **[Auto Retopo](30.md)**, a boolean — drops it, and the mode asks you to Analyze again. Do the geometry work first, then segment.
- The part colours are a **preview palette**. They are assigned in the order the parts appear and are reassigned whenever the part count changes; they mean nothing beyond telling one part from its neighbour.
- Exporting the parts is a good way to work on one of them in isolation: bring a single part back in as its own asset, then **[Auto UV](29.md)**, **[Bake](41.md)** or **[Game-Ready](42.md)** it on its own.

---

Nothing here modifies the mesh in the editor. The only output is the exported GLB, and the analysis itself lives only for as long as the mesh keeps its current topology.
