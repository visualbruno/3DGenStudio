# Assembly

Dress a character. Generate a body and its armour separately — each from its own prompt, each at its own scale — then bring them into one scene, place them by hand, and let the fit adapt each piece to the body underneath. The result saves as a new version of every piece and, if you want it, one merged character ready for **[Auto Rig](32.md)**, **[Animations](34.md)** or export.

Assembly is a **workspace of its own**, reached from **Assembly** in the top navigation. It is not a Mesh Editor mode, because the Mesh Editor works on exactly one mesh and an assembly is a list of independently placed ones.

> Fitting runs on the **Python mesh-tools service**. Configure it in **Settings → Mesh Tools**; the desktop app starts it for you. Placing, sculpting and saving all work without it.

## Assemblies belong to no project

An assembly is **global**. It has no project, and its pieces routinely come from several: a body generated in one project wearing armour generated in another is the normal case, and a project column would forbid it. The mesh picker lists every mesh you have, with a project filter and an **Unassigned** bucket.

The consequence is worth knowing: an assembly does **not** travel with a project export. It lives in the local database, or on the shared server if you have one.

## Setting up

1. **Add meshes** on the left rail, picking as many as you like at once.
2. Make the body the **base** — hover its row and click **Make this the base**. The base is the thing everything else is fitted *to*; every other piece is a *piece*.
3. Each row carries an eye (hide), a lock, an isolate, an opacity slider, wireframe and **X-ray**, which lets you see the pieces through the body.

Meshes load at **true relative scale**. An armour generated at 2% of the body's size looks like it — the **Vs base** read-out in the Measurements panel says so in a number, which is faster than hunting for it with the camera.

## Placing a piece

### Align to base

**Fit to region** is the one click that turns a wildly mis-scaled piece into something wearable. Pick a region — *Whole body*, *Head*, *Torso*, *Hands*, *Feet* — and the piece is moved and scaled onto it.

- **Keep proportions** scales uniformly; **Stretch to fill** matches each axis to the region.
- **Move only** repositions without touching the size.
- Regions are **fixed fractional bands of the base's bounding box** and assume an upright, A- or T-posed body. They are a starting point you then nudge, not a claim of anatomical correctness.

**Drop to surface** slides the piece along its own normal until it rests on the body. **Mirror X** flips it in place, and **Duplicate mirrored** is how a pair of boots or gauntlets happens in one click.

### By hand

The gizmo does **Move (G)**, **Rotate (R)** and **Scale (S)**, in world or local space, with optional snapping. The numeric fields take exact values — rotation in degrees — with a uniform-scale lock. **Copy transform** and **Paste** move a placement from one piece to another; **Reset** returns to the origin.

## Fitting

Select a piece and choose a **material class**. The class is a preset: it sets the stages and their values, and touching any of them switches you to **Custom**.

| Class | For | Chain |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Rigid / Plate** | Plate armour, pauldrons, helms | Seat, then push out, with a generous clearance |
| **Soft / Cloth** | Cloth, chainmail, anything close-fitting | The same, with a tight clearance and more smoothing |
| **Custom** | Your own choices | Whatever you tick |

### The stages

| Stage | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| **Seat on the body** | Moves, turns and resizes the piece **as one solid object** until it stops clipping. Never deforms it, so plate keeps its edges — and because the result is a placement, it appears in the transform fields and undoes like any other move. |
| **Match the landmarks** | Bends the piece so each landmark pair meets. The only stage that can correct **proportions** — a sleeve cut for a longer arm. See [Landmarks](#landmarks). |
| **Reshape to the body** | Conforms the whole piece to the body's silhouette. **Experimental**: on a thick piece it can push the lining through the outer shell. |
| **Push out of the body** | Moves only what is still inside the body back out to the clearance. Everything already clear keeps the shape you gave it. |

**Clearance** is the gap left between piece and body. Not zero on purpose: a garment sitting exactly on the skin z-fights with it and pokes through the moment it animates. **Smoothing** spreads each vertex's correction over its neighbours, which is what stops creases zigzagging.

Two behaviours of **Seat on the body** are worth stating, because both are deliberate:

- A piece that is **already clear** of the body is left exactly where you put it, including the gap you left inside it. It resolves clipping; it does not pull armour onto skin.
- It will **not shrink a piece that is merely too big** and floating. Every rule that pulls a floating piece inward also pulls a correctly-clearanced one inward, and nothing local tells them apart. Use **Fit to region** for gross sizing — it matches extents and has neither failure.

### Preview and revert

A fit is a **preview**. The loaded mesh is never modified, so **Revert** costs nothing, and the A/B toggle on each row switches between Original and Fitted.

Previews **survive leaving the page**. The fitted shape is stored with the assembly and restored when you come back, so navigating to Assets mid-session does not throw the work away. Revert deletes it for good.

> Quality depends heavily on the piece being clean. If a fit comes out lumpy, run **Repair** on that mesh first (in the **[Auto Retopo](30.md)** panel) — a piece whose surfaces already self-intersect cannot be conformed reliably.

## Landmarks

Pairs of points that say *this goes here*: one on the body, one on the piece. Nothing else in the fit knows which part of the piece belongs where, which is why this is the only stage that can fix proportions.

1. Select the piece and click **Place pairs**.
2. Click a point on the **body**, then the matching point on the **piece**. Repeat — the tool stays armed.
3. **Esc** abandons the pair in progress; the ones already placed are kept.

**How you arrange them matters far more than how many there are.** Four well-spread pairs beat twenty in a line. Pairs strung along the piece leave the warp unconstrained sideways, and it responds by swinging wildly in the direction they do not cover — so the fit **refuses** rather than mangling the piece, and tells you by how much. Aim for front, back and both sides, and cover the piece's full length.

A minimum of **four** pairs is required, and they must not all lie on one plane.

## Elastic Grab

The manual tool, for the things no automatic stage gets right. Turn it on and drag the surface; the mesh follows under the cursor with a smooth falloff, like the Blender brush of the same name.

- **Brush size** is a screen radius, shown as a ring.
- Sculpting edits the same preview a fit produces, so **Revert** still undoes everything.
- The transform gizmo is **hidden** while the brush is active, and **G/R/S** are ignored — the handles sit in front of the mesh and would swallow the stroke. **Esc** leaves the brush.

## Saving

Nothing becomes an asset until you press **Save as assets**. The two outputs are independent.

### Fitted piece versions

Each edited piece is saved as a **new version of its own asset** — a child on the [Assets](7.md) page, inheriting that asset's projects, with the original untouched. The version is written in the piece's **own local space**, so it drops back into any assembly the way the original did, and the piece in this assembly is re-pointed at it so the fit is there next time you open the document.

### The assembled character

One mesh with each piece as its own node, in world space. It is saved as a **library asset with no project**, which is where an assembly's output belongs when its inputs came from several — with an optional **Add to project** if you want it somewhere specific.

#### Transfer skin weights from the base

If the base is rigged, every piece can be bound to **one copy of its skeleton**, so the whole character animates together. Any animations the base carries come along too.

This is also the way to avoid a trap: rigging the merged mesh by taking it through the Mesh Editor **flattens its materials**, because the editor is single-mesh and single-material by construction. Transfer the weights here instead and you never need that round trip.

#### Delete body faces the armour hides

Removes the parts of the body nobody can see. A face goes only when **every** direction from it is blocked by a piece, with a one-ring margin kept at the edges, and only pieces count — never the body hiding itself, or you would lose the armpits.

> Judged at the **rest pose**. Armour slides against skin as a character moves, so a face hidden standing still can open into a hole mid-animation. If your base is animated, the dialog warns you — check the result in the Animations tab.

#### Merge into one texture and UV layout

Packs every piece's UVs into one shared atlas and bakes their textures into it, so the character arrives as one mesh, one material, one texture.

| Field | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| **Texture size** | 1024 to 8192. Bigger fits more detail before anything has to shrink. |
| **At most** | How many textures you will accept — 1, 2 or 4. Only as many as needed are made. |

Each piece keeps its **own texel density** when there is room, so a 2K armour stays sharper than a 1K boot. When the pieces need more space than the budget allows, everything is scaled down by **one shared factor** rather than some pieces losing more than others — the result panel reports the packing and whether detail was scaled.

> This is **one-way**: the pieces stop being separately addressable, so you can no longer hide or replace the pauldrons on their own. Save the per-node version too if you might want that. Cut-out transparency is not carried into the shared texture, and the dialog says so when a piece uses it.

## Notes

- Fitting works on **static geometry only** — a piece that fits perfectly at rest can still clip at 90° of shoulder rotation, because nothing here is pose-aware.
- A piece whose proportions genuinely differ from the body's cannot be fixed by seating alone; that is what landmarks are for, and even they are only as good as where you click.
- Deleting hidden faces leaves the body **not watertight**, which matters if you later want collision hulls or a 3D print. Do it last.
- An assembly tolerates a piece whose asset has been deleted: the row is flagged rather than the page breaking.
- Assemblies have no owner. On a shared server, everyone using it sees the same list.

---

Placement and sculpting change nothing outside the assembly. Fits live as previews that survive between sessions, and the only thing that writes to your asset library is **Save as assets**.
